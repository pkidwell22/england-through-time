# Vessels on the London Thames, 4000 BCE to 1625: research notes

Workstream: VESSELS (November 2026). This file holds the findings behind `data/locations/london/vessels.json` and the
river-craft entries in `data/locations/london/landmarks.json`. Source ids in brackets are in
`data/locations/london/sources.json` (prefix `ves-`) or `data/sources.json`.

Method. Three research passes (one per period) were run with web search and page fetching, followed by targeted
checks. Only pages actually fetched and read are cited. PDFs from the Archaeology Data Service (Marsden 1965 on the
County Hall ship, Granger on Blackfriars 3) and Milne 2005 were fetched and their text extracted locally. Wikipedia
pages were used as leads; where one is cited it is marked "lead only" in its source title and the claim it supports is
flagged below. Several standard sources refused automated fetching (Historic England, British Museum, British History
Online, the Newport Ship project site, Royal Museums Greenwich galleon page) or returned only scans.

## Prehistory (to c. 50 CE)

- Very few traces of prehistoric boats are known from the greater Thames, possibly for reasons of survival or chance
  [ves-gresham-early-thames].
- Logboats. The London-area example named there is a dugout from a drainage ditch on Erith Marshes (Spurrell, 1885),
  undated, a "run-about" for creeks [ves-gresham-early-thames]. Upriver, the Bourne End logboat was 7.62 m long,
  0.99 m wide, 0.38 m deep, with three carved ribs or seats, an upturned pointed bow and a square stern; it was drawn
  in 1872 and lost by 1880, and is dated only by period attribution (Bronze/Iron Age?) [ves-bucks-her-bourne-end].
  Must Farm (Fenland, not Thames) gives dated comparanda, 1300-400 BCE [ves-intarch-logboats, ves-citizan-prehistoric-boats].
- Sewn-plank boats. Dover (1575-1520 cal BCE, c. 14 m reconstructed), Ferriby 1-3 (c. 2030-1680 cal BCE) and
  Caldicot have a coastal distribution; no example is known from the Thames [ves-intarch-sewn-plank, ves-citizan-prehistoric-boats].
- Cunobelin's coins (10-40 CE) show high-sided sailing merchantmen, probably familiar in estuarine waters
  [ves-gresham-early-thames] (pictorial; not a London find).
- **Decision.** Nothing is evidenced in this reach. Two logboats (one drawn up on each shore) stand for sparse local
  use from 2350 BCE (the start of the Bourne End period attribution; nothing is shown before it); no sewn-plank boat and no Iron Age plank ship is shown. Logboats are not shown after the
  Roman conquest (the type persisted elsewhere in Britain, but not as a common craft here).

## Roman (c. 50-410)

- **Blackfriars ship 1.** Dendro-dated about AD 150, sank soon after; found 1962 in the Thames bed off Blackfriars
  (upstream of the Roman bridge). Oak, frame-first, flush-laid planks nailed to frames, no keel but two broad keel
  planks; flat-bottomed; mast step a rectangular socket in a floor timber about one third of the length from the bow,
  with a coin of Domitian beneath; cargo about 26 tons of Kentish ragstone [ves-mass-blackfriars1,
  ves-lm-blackfriars-maststep]. Length 16-17 m, beam about 6 m (14 m survived) [ves-mass-blackfriars1]. Milne: one of a
  large fleet of stone barges needed for the city's building programmes [ves-milne-port-roman-london].
  Steering gear did not survive (not verified in any source read); the single square sail is the usual inference from
  the mast step.
- **New Guy's House boat** (Southwark creek, 1958): smaller vessel of the same Romano-Celtic build (iron spikes driven
  through planks and frames and turned over like staples) [ves-milne-port-roman-london]; flat-bottomed river barge
  about 16 x 4 m [ves-hol-roman-port, secondary]; abandoned about AD 200 and whether it carried a mast were seen only in
  search snippets (not verified), so no mast is drawn.
- **County Hall ship** (Lambeth, 1910). Marsden 1965 [ves-marsden-county-hall]: carvel-built, keeled, planks
  edge-joined with "draw-tongued joints" (tenons) in the Mediterranean manner, shell-first; oak of a northern European
  species, so built in the north by a builder who knew the Mediterranean method; estimated 60-70 ft long, 15-16 ft beam,
  at least 6 ft deep; probably decked; propelled by sail (pulley block, possible mast fragment, no rowing
  arrangement); sank after 293 (coins of Carausius and Allectus in the bottom); about 59 tons, the smallest class of
  Roman merchant ship. Milne gives construction about AD 285 and a 3 m beam [ves-milne-port-roman-london] (conflicts
  with Marsden's beam; Marsden's figure is used).
- **Port chronology.** Harbour works from about AD 62-3, a possible ferry landing stage about AD 70, the timber bridge by
  85-90 [ves-gresham-port-growth]; harbour maintained to about AD 250 and allowed to collapse thereafter; 3rd-century
  quays deliberately dismantled; the late 3rd-century riverside wall cut the town off from the river; the riverside
  wall reused monuments because the fleet that had carried fresh stone was no longer available
  [ves-milne-port-roman-london].
- **Not evidenced.** No warship is evidenced at London; CLBR-stamped tiles of the Classis Britannica are known from
  Dover, and none was found linked to London in the sources read. None of the Roman vessels found in northern Europe
  was Mediterranean-built [ves-hol-roman-port]. No small Roman boat has been found at London (skiffs and ferries are
  inferred).
- **Decision.** Four types (Blackfriars barge, New Guy's House barge, County Hall merchantman, conjectural skiff),
  50-410; the mix thins at 250 and again at 300; nothing after 410.

## 5th-9th centuries

- No boat has been found at Lundenwic. The London Museum: trade from about 600, settlement from about 650, first
  recorded Viking raid 842, abandoned by the 880s; riverside embankments of wood and wattle excavated in 1988; "Strand"
  means beach or bank; Bede calls Lundenwic a trading centre for many nations who visit it by land and sea
  [ves-lm-lundenwic].
- Graveney boat (Kent marshes near Faversham, not the Thames proper): dendro about AD 895 (radiocarbon late 9th c.),
  over 14 m long, under 3 m beam, clinker with iron rivets, no sign of a mast, a probable sea-going trader possibly
  built in southern or eastern England [ves-kent-her-graveney]. The commonly quoted "10th century" date and a cargo of
  hops and quernstones were NOT supported by the record read.
- Sutton Hoo (620s): about 27 m, clinker, oared [ves-wp-sutton-hoo, lead only]; the sail is unverified.
- **Decision.** Nothing at the Roman bridgehead from 410 until the late Saxon revival (900). Lundenwic: three
  Graveney-type traders and four small boats drawn up on the Strand shore, 650-870, oared, side-steered, no masts.

## Viking Age and late Saxon (c. 850-1066)

- Skuldelev comparanda (Viking Ship Museum, Roskilde): Skuldelev 2, built 1042 near Dublin, 29.3 x 3.8 m, 60 oars,
  crew 65-70 [ves-vsm-skuldelev2]; Skuldelev 5, c. 1030, 17.3 x 2.5 m, 26 oars, crew about 30, 46 m2 sail
  [ves-vsm-skuldelev5]; Skuldelev 3, c. 1040, 14 x 3.3 m, 45 m2 sail, 4.6 t cargo [ves-vsm-skuldelev3]; Skuldelev 1,
  c. 1030, ocean-going cargo ship [ves-vsm-skuldelev1].
- Billingsgate tolls (c. 978-1016, possibly later): a small ship one halfpenny, a larger ship with a sail one penny,
  a "long ship" or barge, fish boats, the ships of Rouen [ves-fordham-billingsgate-tolls]. The translation read does
  not use the words hulc or ceol.
- Reused clinker planking is common in London's medieval waterfront revetments (e.g. Three Quays, 14th c.)
  [ves-mola-three-quays]; specific Saxon or Viking timbers at named sites were NOT verified.
- **1014.** The attack on London Bridge is saga tradition (sources already in the dataset). The staging now shows
  seven longships of the Skuldelev 5 and 2 types, rowing, sails furled, below the timber bridge; it stays conjectural.

## 1066-1400

- Stern rudder. The Ipswich common seal (1200) shows the earliest representation of a rudder hung on the sternpost by
  pintles and gudgeons [ves-rmg-ipswich-seal]. The Winchelsea seal (early 14th c.) still shows a quarter rudder; the Rye
  seal of about 1390 shows gudgeons for a stern rudder [ves-rye-seals]. Transition taken as 1200-1300+: no stern rudder
  before 1200, no side-rudder sea-going type after 1300.
- Cogs: flush-laid flat bottom amidships, clinker sides, long straight stem and stern posts; side rudder replaced by a
  sternpost rudder from about 1200; decked and with a stern castle on larger vessels from the 13th century; 15-25 m;
  oldest depiction the Lübeck seal of 1223; the Bremen cog of the 1380s [ves-wp-cog, ves-wp-bremen-cog, leads only;
  the DSM paper could not be read]. Cologne merchants had a hall at Dowgate from 1157; the Hanse merchants formed one
  London community in the later 13th c. [ves-hol-hanse]. No cog-specific London record was found, so cogs are shown
  1250-1450 on the strength of the Hanseatic trade, as "reconstructed, low".
- Hulks: no surviving hull can be matched to the written "hulk"; not modelled as a separate type.
- Blackfriars 3: built locally 1380-1415 (dendro), sank late 15th c., 14.64 x 4.3 x 0.88 m, clinker-built sailing
  vessel, identified as the shout, "the popular 14th- and 15th-century river transporter" [ves-granger-blackfriars3].
  Found between Trig Lane and Blackfriars, i.e. above the bridge.
- A boat reused in a Southwark fish tank, dendro 1260-70, with oar holes (possibly a small galley or passenger barge)
  [ves-lm-fishtank-boat]; its recorded dimensions conflict, so it is not used for a model.
- Larger ships moored mid-stream and were discharged by lighter [ves-erenow-port-history, secondary].
- Not found: shouts other than Blackfriars 3, Peter boats, the London common seal ship, Custom House and Abbots Lane
  boats. These are not shown as types.

## 1400-1625

- Drawbridge. 1481: the Drawbridge Tower damaged by the vibration of carts and by raising and lowering the drawbridge
  [ves-moeml-drawbridge]; by 1497 the drawbridge was judged too dangerous to use and large boats could no longer reach
  Queenhithe; it was made workable once more in 1500 for Henry VII's barque; tower rebuilt 1577
  [ves-hol-tudor-bridge, ves-moeml-drawbridge]. Stow: once ships came to Queenhithe when the bridge was drawn up; in
  Edward IV's reign the market there was hindered by the slackness of drawing up the bridge [ves-stow-queenhithe].
  **Rule:** before 1500 a few tall ships are placed above the bridge (Queenhithe, Steelyard); from 1500 every craft
  that cannot pass the arches is below it, except the western barges that worked the upper river.
- Carvel. The Newport ship (1450s) is clinker, about 35 m [ves-wp-newport-ship, lead only]; the Mary Rose (1510-12)
  carvel, keel about 32 m, four masts, high castles [ves-wp-mary-rose, lead only; ves-maryrose-ship]; by 1545 ships
  built on the Thames used the newer construction [ves-hol-tudor-port]. Carracks shown 1470-1580, in the Pool from 1500.
- The Gresham ship (Thames estuary): English oak felled in or after 1574, carvel, 24.5 m overall, 19 m keel, 7.4 m
  beam, hold 3.4 m deep, gunports, a typical armed merchantman matching many London ships [ves-gresham-ship]. This
  is the basis of the 1560-1625 merchant ship. Galleon rig and the "race-built" lower upperworks from
  [ves-wp-galleon, lead only].
- Hoy: "hoye of Dorderycht" in a Paston letter of 1495 [ves-wp-hoy, lead only]; sprit rig date NOT verified.
- Wherries and watermen: watermen first recorded in the 1514 fare statute; the Company of Watermen created 1555
  [ves-hol-watermen]; the 1555 Act set a wherry at 22.5 ft x 4.5 ft amidships [ves-oocities-watermen, amateur site
  citing Humpherus]; Stow 1598: above 2,000 wherries and other small boats, at least 3,000 watermen at work
  [ves-stow-waters]. The often quoted "40,000 watermen" (John Taylor) includes dependants and is propaganda; the
  Admiralty census of 1629 gives 2,426 [ves-oocities-watermen].
- Tilt-boats: the Gravesend-Billingsgate long ferry from the 13th c.; regulation after complaints in 1562
  [ves-kas-tilt-boats]; the tilt-boat as such from the mid 16th c. (a museum caption and the Essex Field Club, seen in
  search results only); earliest model seen about 1650. Shown 1560-1625, below the bridge, low confidence.
- State and livery barges: the earliest account of a water procession on Lord Mayor's Day in the Brewers' records of
  1422; royal rowbarges typically 60-75 ft with a canopied house; Tudor coronation processions by barge
  [ves-gloriana-rowbarges]. Norman's procession of 1453 is the commonly cited first (seen in search results only).
- Western barges, dung boats, Peter boats: only Stow's "boats, lighters and barges" at Queenhithe was verified
  [ves-stow-queenhithe]; western barges are shown with a reconstructed form and low confidence; dung boats and Peter
  boats are not shown.
- Dutch eel ships: the only verified date is the 1699 Act (eels sold only by Dutch fishermen in boats moored in the
  Thames) [ves-cityoflondon-billingsgate]; a pre-1625 date was not verified, so they are NOT shown.
- Panoramas (Wyngaerde c. 1544, Agas c. 1561, Norden 1600, Visscher 1616): no text describing their river craft could
  be fetched; the above/below-bridge contrast rests on the drawbridge evidence above, not on a reading of the views.

## What could not be verified

Steering of any Romano-Celtic ship; a mast on the New Guy's House boat; the sail on Sutton Hoo and Graveney; Saxon or
Viking clinker timbers at named London sites; any cog at London by name; hulks; Peter boats, dung boats, shouts other
than Blackfriars 3; the sprit rig before 1625; the first tilt-boat; western barge form; eel ships before 1699; what the
panoramas show of river craft; hull colours of any period (only the Blackfriars caulking of hazel shavings in pine
resin is attested).
